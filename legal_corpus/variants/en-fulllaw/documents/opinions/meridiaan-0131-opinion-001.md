### 1. Introduction and Background

We refer to our instructions regarding the application for a temporary residence permit (*machtiging tot voorlopig verblijf*) submitted by Brightlane Software B.V. on behalf of its director, Mr. Aris Thorne (hereinafter: "the Client"). The application was filed on 10 January 2025, citing grounds under Article 29 of the Aliens Act 2000 (*Vreemdelingenwet 2000*) regarding international protection, alongside supplementary arguments based on Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) concerning family life.

The Immigration and Naturalisation Service (IND) issued a rejection decision on 3 May 2025 (reference: IND/2025/9941/BLS). The primary ground for rejection was that the Client did not meet the strict criteria for refugee status or subsidiary protection under Article 29 of the Aliens Act 2000. The IND acknowledged the existence of family ties in the Netherlands but deemed them insufficient to outweigh the public interest in removal, given the lack of recognized international protection status.

This opinion evaluates the prospects of success on appeal before the administrative law division of the Council of State (*Raad van State*), specifically focusing on a hybrid legal track that leverages the Article 8 ECHR claim as a robust supplementary basis for residence, effectively bypassing the high threshold of Article 29 Aliens Act 2000.

### 2. Legal Framework and Analysis

#### 2.1 The Limitations of the Article 29 Aliens Act 2000 Track

The IND’s rejection relied on a strict interpretation of Article 29 Aliens Act 2000, which implements the Qualification Directive. To succeed under this provision, one must demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution due to race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, or face a real risk of serious harm.

The IND determined that Mr. Thorne’s business practices in his country of origin (Turkey), while politically sensitive, did not reach the threshold of "persecution" required by Dutch administrative law precedents. Furthermore, the internal flight alternative was deemed available. While we can challenge the factual findings regarding the risk assessment, the procedural history indicates that courts are generally deferential to the IND’s credibility assessments in complex political cases unless there is clear new evidence. Consequently, relying solely on Article 29 Aliens Act 2000 presents a moderate to low probability of success on appeal.

#### 2.2 The Strength of the Article 8 ECHR Family-Life Track

The stronger legal avenue lies in the independent assessment of Article 8 ECHR (Right to respect for private and family life). Mr. Thorne has been resident in the Netherlands since 15 March 2022. He is married to Ms. Elara Vane, a Dutch national residing in Amsterdam at Keizersgracht 123, Amsterdam. They have one minor child, born on 12 August 2023, who holds dual nationality.

Under the jurisprudence of the European Court of Human Rights and Dutch case law (e.g., *Yildiz v. the Netherlands*), the assessment under Article 8 requires a balancing of interests:
1. The severity of the interference with family life if the Client is removed.
2. The solidity of social, cultural, and family ties in both countries.

In this matter, the ties in the Netherlands are profound and recent but significant. The minor child’s best interests are paramount. The IND failed to adequately conduct a fresh proportionality test focusing on the *individual* circumstances of the child’s upbringing and the spouse’s dependency, instead relying on a general presumption that business immigration applications do not automatically trigger Article 8 protections.

By decoupling the residence claim from the strict international protection criteria and framing it as an exceptional Article 8 case due to the presence of a minor Dutch/EU citizen child and a long-standing partnership, we create a viable path for residence on humanitarian grounds. This track is particularly effective because it shifts the burden: the IND must demonstrate that the public interest in expulsion outweighs the specific family disruption, which is difficult when a young child is involved.

#### 2.3 Strategic Synthesis

The strongest track is not to abandon the Article 29 claim entirely but to subordinate it. The appeal should argue that even if Article 29 Aliens Act 2000 is not met, the procedural error in the IND's Article 8 assessment constitutes a sufficient ground for annulment. We will request the Council of State to rule on the Article 8 claim de novo, given that the lower instance failed to properly weigh the factors under Article 8 ECHR.

The argument is structured as follows:
1. The rejection under Article 29 Aliens Act 2000 is acknowledged but contested on factual grounds regarding the risk profile.
2. However, the primary request for relief is based on Article 8 ECHR.
3. The existence of a minor child and spousal ties creates a family life that cannot be severed without disproportionate hardship.
4. The IND did not sufficiently consider the possibility of a residence permit being issued in light of these Article 8 obligations, as permitted under the *Aliens Circular* (Vreemdelingencirculaire) section B12/6.

### 3. Procedural Considerations

The appeal must be filed no later than **14 July 2025** at the Council of State in The Hague. Given the complexity, we recommend submitting a detailed statement of grounds (*ambtsbericht*) accompanied by new medical and psychological reports regarding the minor child’s attachment to Mr. Thorne, dated 1 June 2025, to reinforce the current reality of the family life.

We must also address the public interest argument. Brightlane Software B.V. contributes significantly to the Dutch tech sector. While this is not a direct legal ground under Article 8 ECHR, it serves as contextual evidence supporting the proportionality of granting residence, demonstrating that removal would cause unnecessary economic and social disruption beyond the family unit.

### 4. Conclusion and Recommendation

The prospects of success on appeal are **moderate to high** if we pivot the core legal argument to Article 8 ECHR. The strict application of Article 29 Aliens Act 2000 remains a hurdle, but it is surmountable when combined with the compelling family-life arguments.

We recommend:
1. Filing the appeal by **14 July 2025**.
2. Emphasizing the Article 8 ECHR claim as the primary basis for the request for a residence permit on humanitarian grounds.
3. Providing concrete evidence of the minor child’s needs and the spousal relationship's solidity.

The IND’s rejection order will be challenged not merely as an error in assessing persecution risk, but as a failure to properly balance the right to family life under Article 8 ECHR against the state’s immigration policy. This approach aligns with recent precedents where residence was granted despite the lack of refugee status, due to exceptional family circumstances.

We await your instructions to draft the formal appeal petition.
